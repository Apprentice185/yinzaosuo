PVC foam board — the rigid, lightweight plastic sheet also sold as foamed PVC or Sintra-type board (and known as 雪弗板 in China) — is the default material for cut letters, reception logos, exhibition panels and retail displays. It prints well, routes cleanly, and weighs far less than timber or aluminium. But "PVC foam board" on a quote can mean very different sheets: soft low-density board that dents under a fingernail, or hard high-density board that holds a routed edge for years. This guide walks through the five choices that decide whether the finished sign looks right and lasts — thickness, density, surface finish, sheet size, and how the graphics are applied.

If you are still deciding between board types, our earlier comparison of [KT board, foam core and PVC foam board](/articles/kt-board-vs-foam-board-vs-pvc-foam-board/) covers how the three materials differ. This article assumes you have settled on PVC foam board and need to specify it properly.

## The short answer

For most indoor signage, a matte white board in the middle of the density range does the job: around 3–5 mm for printed panels, 10 mm and up for cut letters. Go thicker and denser when the sign is large, freestanding, outdoors, or expected to last for years. Go thinner and lighter only when the panel is fully supported, handled gently, and short-lived. Every dimension below follows from those trade-offs.

## 1. Thickness: match it to size and stiffness

Foamed PVC is sold across a wide thickness range — roughly 1 mm to 30 mm in the general market — but sign work concentrates in a few bands:

- **1–3 mm** — thin, semi-flexible sheets. Used for small printed panels, backing boards, and graphics that will be fully mounted onto a wall or another substrate. Too flexible to stand on its own at any real size.
- **5 mm** — the general-purpose signage thickness. Rigid enough for medium printed panels, menu boards and point-of-sale displays, and a common choice for direct UV printing.
- **10 mm** — the starting point for cut letters and logos with real depth, deeper routed work, and larger panels that must stay flat without a frame.
- **15–20 mm and above** — chunky dimensional letters, freestanding display elements, and applications where the edge of the board is part of the look.

The rule underneath: stiffness rises fast with thickness, so a large panel in 3 mm will bow and flutter where the same panel in 10 mm stays flat. If a quote looks surprisingly cheap for a big sign, check the thickness first — it is the easiest specification to quietly downgrade.

## 2. Density: the specification that decides hardness

Density is the least visible and most consequential choice. Foamed PVC is made by expanding PVC into a cellular core; how much material is left in that structure sets the board's hardness, weight and price.

- **Lower-density board** is lighter and cheaper, but the surface dents more easily, routed edges come out rougher, and screws or fixings have less to bite into. Fine for short-term indoor graphics and fully supported panels.
- **Higher-density board** has a harder skin, crisper routed and cut edges, better screw-holding, and more resistance to knocks in transit and on site. It is the safer choice for cut letters, long-term signage, and anything that will be handled repeatedly, such as exhibition kits.

Two boards of identical thickness and size can differ noticeably in weight for this reason — when comparing quotes, weight per sheet is a useful cross-check that the density is comparable.

A related distinction is how the surface is formed. **Free-foam board** has a softer, more matte skin. **Celuka (integrally skinned) board** is formed with a harder, denser crust on the faces, which gives a smoother print surface and better impact resistance. For premium retail work and letters, the celuka type is usually worth specifying.

## 3. Surface and finish: set it up for the graphics

- **Matte white** is the standard face for direct UV flatbed printing — the matte surface controls reflections and shows printed colour evenly. It is the safe default for almost all printed panels.
- **Gloss** surfaces look sharp unprinted but show every dent, scratch and reflection once installed; they are rarely the right choice under retail lighting.
- **Coloured board** earns its keep for cut letters and logos, where the colour runs through the edge as well as the face, so no paint is needed for a clean result.

However the face is finished, decide the graphics method alongside it (section 5) — a board chosen for direct printing is not always the board you would choose for applied vinyl or paint.

## 4. Sheet size and joining: plan the panel before production

The most common sheet size in the trade is 1220 × 2440 mm (4 × 8 ft), and most signage pricing and nesting assumes it. Designing panels to nest within standard sheets keeps offcut waste — and cost — down. Oversize graphics are handled in one of three ways:

- **Tiled panels** — the artwork is split across sheets with aligned joins; standard practice for exhibition walls and large hoardings.
- **A backing structure** — battens or a frame behind thinner board adds stiffness, often cheaper than jumping several thickness grades.
- **A thicker single sheet** — cleanest result, highest material and freight cost.

For Australian trade buyers ordering from China, sheet size also decides freight efficiency: full sheets travel flat-packed and stack densely, which is one reason board products ship economically by sea. Sea freight to main Australian ports typically takes around 40 days in transit, so panel planning is worth doing before, not after, the order is placed. ## 5. Printing and fabrication: what the board will go through

- **Direct UV flatbed printing** is the standard route for printed panels — fast, full-colour, and the ink bonds well to a clean matte face. Ask for a matte protective lamination or UV-stable ink set if the panel will sit under strong light.
- **Applied vinyl graphics** work well on smooth, harder-skinned board; on soft board, vinyl removal can tear the face.
- **CNC routing and cutting** is how letters and shapes are made. Denser board gives cleaner edges and finer detail; very low-density board can crumble at small letter sizes.
- **Painting** is common for letters — the face takes spray paint well once dust is removed, and edges can be painted to match or contrast.
- **Heat forming and bending** are possible within limits, which is why the same board appears in curved display elements — confirm bend radii with the producer before designing tight curves.

## Common mistakes

1. **Specifying thickness but not density.** Two 5 mm boards can perform very differently. Name both in your brief.
2. **Using thin board for a large unframed panel.** It will bow. Add thickness, a backing, or tiling.
3. **Choosing gloss for printed graphics under lights.** Reflections kill legibility; matte is the working default.
4. **Assuming all white board prints the same.** Old stock, dusty faces and very soft skins all show up in the print. A printed proof is cheap insurance on a large run.
5. **Designing letters too small for the thickness.** Denser board and sensible minimum stroke widths keep letters intact through routing, packing and installation.

## When PVC foam board is not the right choice

- **Very short-life, lowest-cost indoor posters and presentations** — paper-faced foam board (KT board) is cheaper and lighter, and entirely adequate while it is handled gently and kept dry. Our [PVC foam board range](/products/ys-pvc-foam-board/) and the comparison article linked above can help you place each material.
- **Outdoor site boards that will be knocked, screwed and weather-beaten** — corrugated plastic (corflute) is cheaper to replace, and for permanent exterior fascia signs, aluminium composite panel is tougher and more heat-stable than foamed PVC.
- **Hot environments and strong all-day sun** — foamed PVC softens at high temperatures and large dark panels can warp in harsh sun. For long-term outdoor signs in Australian summer conditions, discuss placement, colour and thickness carefully, or step up to a more heat-resistant substrate.
- **Structural or load-bearing parts** — foam board is a graphic substrate, not a building material.

## Specifying your order

A workable brief names: application and indoor/outdoor placement, finished size, thickness, density expectation (or the performance you need — "cut letters, handled often, must hold crisp edges"), surface/colour, graphics method, and quantity. The exact grades, sheet sizes and options available for your order are **confirmed in your quotation** before production — send the brief via the [PVC foam board product page](/products/ys-pvc-foam-board/) and the specification can be pinned down from there.
