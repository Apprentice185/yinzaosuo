A printed poster looks perfect the day it comes off the printer. Then someone touches it, rain gets at it, the sun fades it, and a month later it looks tired. Cold lamination film is what stands between those two states: a clear protective layer pressed onto the print — no heat involved — that takes the scratches, moisture and UV instead of the ink underneath. Choosing it is mostly three decisions: finish, thickness, and whether the graphic will live on a wall or under people's feet.

## First, what "cold" actually means

Cold lamination film uses a pressure-sensitive adhesive. It bonds when rollers press it onto the print at room temperature — no heated nip, no warm-up. That matters for one reason above all: heat-sensitive media. Photographs, inkjet prints and some vinyls warp, curl or change under heat, and cold lamination leaves them alone. It is also the standard way to finish adhesive-backed media such as self-adhesive vinyl graphics and floor decals, because only one side of the graphic gets laminated — the printed face.

Hot (thermal) lamination is the alternative: a heat-activated adhesive, faster and usually cheaper, and fine for ordinary paper-based prints. It is not the subject of this guide, and it is not interchangeable with cold film — the two need different machines and suit different media. If a supplier quotes "lamination" without saying which, ask.

## The short answer

- **Gloss** for colour pop: retail posters, photos, promotional displays where the picture is the point.
- **Matte** for readability: signs under bright lights, exhibition graphics, anything people read up close or photograph.
- **Floor laminate** for anything people walk on — and only a laminate rated anti-slip. Ordinary gloss or matte film on a floor is a slip hazard, not a finish.
- **Thickness** follows the job: thin film for short-term indoor prints, thicker film for handled or outdoor work, and a much thicker textured film for floors. Exact thickness, roll width and price are confirmed per order — see the ordering checklist at the end.

## One: gloss vs matte — the finish decision

This is the decision most buyers get wrong by treating it as taste. It is really about light.

**Gloss** makes colours look deeper and more saturated, and the surface wipes clean easily. The price is reflection: under spotlights, near windows or under camera flashes, a gloss print throws glare back at the viewer, and fingerprints show on it. It is the default for photo-quality posters, retail promotions and display graphics where impact matters more than reading.

**Matte** scatters light instead of reflecting it, so the graphic stays readable from any angle and photographs without hot spots. Colours look very slightly flatter — the film trades a little vibrancy for readability — and scuffs can show more visibly on a matte surface. It is the default for exhibition panels, menu boards, wayfinding signs and any graphic under strong lighting.

**Satin** sits between the two — a soft sheen, less glare than gloss, more colour than matte — and while gloss and matte are the standard finishes supplied here, satin can be arranged on request.

| Finish | Look | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gloss | Deep colour, shiny | Retail posters, photos, promotions | Glare under lights; fingerprints show |
| Matte | Flat, no reflection | Exhibitions, menus, signs, readable graphics | Colours slightly flatter; scuffs show |
| Satin (on request) | Soft sheen | Display graphics that need both | Ask when quoting; judge the sheen on a sample |

A practical test beats a description: laminate the same print in gloss and matte, put both under the actual lighting where the graphic will hang, and look at them from the distance the audience will stand. The right finish is usually obvious within seconds.

## Two: thickness — how much protection is enough

Film thickness is measured in microns (µm). For wide-format overlaminates, the films on the market commonly sit around 70 µm in total thickness — for example, a widely sold premium PVC overlaminate is 2.75 mil (about 70 µm), and a solvent-based pressure-sensitive laminate range lists 70 µm as its standard. That is a market reference point, not a specification for any one supplier's film: the thickness actually supplied, along with roll width, length and price, is confirmed per order.

The principle is simple:

- **Thin film** for short-term indoor prints: posters, event graphics, anything that will be replaced within weeks. It is more flexible, easier to apply, and cheaper.
- **Thicker film** for handled, outdoor or long-term work: menu boards, retail signs, graphics people touch. It resists scratches and edge wear better, but it is stiffer and less forgiving on curves.
- **Much thicker, textured film** for floors — see the next section. Floor laminate is not just "thicker gloss film".

Thicker is not automatically better. A thick film on a thin, flexible print can make the finished piece stiff and prone to curling at the edges; a thin film on a floor graphic will wear through in days. Match the film to the job, not to the thickest option on the list.

## Three: floor laminate is a different product

Floor graphics fail in two ways that wall graphics never do: people slip on them, and foot traffic grinds them away. A floor laminate therefore has to do two jobs an ordinary laminate does not:

1. **Anti-slip surface.** The film has a textured surface rated for slip resistance — for example, one established floor laminate is rated R10 for anti-slip, with a 170 µm face film (200 µm total thickness) and a stated durability of around 2 years. That rating belongs to that product; other floor laminates carry their own ratings, and an unrated film should never be assumed safe for floors.
2. **Wear resistance.** It has to survive shoes, grit, cleaning machines and trolleys, not just fingers.

Ordinary gloss or matte cold lamination film is not a floor finish, however thick it is. If a graphic will be walked on — retail floors, exhibitions, wayfinding, events — specify a dedicated floor laminate with a stated slip rating, and confirm the rating in writing. This is a safety decision, not a cosmetic one.

## Four: UV protection and outdoor life

A good overlaminate slows fading: UV inhibitors in the film or its adhesive absorb the ultraviolet that bleaches ink. Some premium overlaminates claim to extend a print's outdoor life by up to three additional years when used with compatible media and properly cured inks — that is one manufacturer's claim for its own product, not a general promise.

Outdoor life in practice depends on the ink system, the base media, the climate, the direction the graphic faces and whether it is laminated at all. A laminated print in a shaded doorway lasts far longer than the same print facing afternoon sun. Treat any durability number as product-specific: ask what it was tested with, and confirm it for the actual media and ink combination before ordering in volume.

## Five: compatibility — the step people skip

Cold lamination only works if the film's adhesive likes the print underneath. Before a production run:

- **Match the ink system.** Solvent, eco-solvent, latex and UV-cured inks behave differently under laminate; a film that bonds well to one can lift or silver on another. Confirm the combination, ideally with a sample.
- **Watch for silvering.** Tiny air bubbles trapped under the film show as a silvery haze, worst over dark, solid colours. It is usually an application problem — dust, low pressure, cold adhesive — and heat-assist lamination (gentle warmth, well below hot lamination temperatures) softens the adhesive and helps prevent it. If silvering appears on a sample, change the process before running the job, not after.
- **Mind the temperature.** Pressure-sensitive adhesive needs a minimum application temperature to bond properly — one floor laminate, for example, specifies a minimum of +10 °C. Cold workshops in winter are a real cause of lifting edges.
- **Test adhesion, then wait.** A bond that looks fine at the roller can lift overnight. Check a sample after 24 hours, including at the edges and over any joins, before committing to the full run.

A small sample run costs little and catches almost every compatibility problem. A full run of laminated prints that will not stay stuck costs the whole job.

## Six: application — what good lamination looks like

- **Clean first.** Dust under a laminate becomes a permanent bubble. The print surface and the work area should be clean, and the film should go on in one steady pass — stopping and restarting invites wrinkles and trapped air.
- **Steady pressure, no stretching.** The film should be laid flat under even roller pressure. Stretching it to fit creates tension that later pulls back and lifts the edges.
- **Small pieces can go on by hand.** For a single poster or board, the hinge method works without a machine: peel back only a small strip of the release liner, anchor that sticky edge to the print, then squeegee from the anchored edge outward in firm, overlapping strokes with a felt squeegee while pulling the liner away gradually underneath. Peeling the whole liner off first is exactly how air and misalignment get trapped.
- **Trim after bonding, not before.** Laminate slightly oversize, let the bond set, then trim to the finished size. Trimming first leaves exposed print edges that catch and peel.
- **Flat surfaces only for standard film.** Standard cold lamination film is made for flat prints. Deep curves, heavily textured surfaces and sharp folds need a conformable film specified for the purpose — confirm before ordering.
- **Store rolls properly.** Film stored damp, hot or crushed will not laminate cleanly, however good the specification was.

## Common mistakes

1. **Choosing gloss for a sign people have to read.** Under bright lights the message disappears into glare; matte was the right call.
2. **Using ordinary laminate on a floor.** It is a slip hazard. Floor graphics need a rated anti-slip laminate.
3. **Assuming any laminate suits any ink.** Solvent, latex and UV prints each need a confirmed combination — test first.
4. **Buying on thickness alone.** The thickest film on a short-term poster is wasted money; the thinnest film on a handled sign is a false economy.
5. **Skipping the sample.** Silvering, lifting and poor adhesion all show up on a sample; on a full run they show up on the invoice.
6. **Laminating over perforated film with the wrong product.** One-way vision film, for example, needs its own dedicated transparent overlaminate — ordinary film can block the holes and spoil the see-through effect. See [Article #4](/articles/one-way-vision-film-perforation-guide/).

## When not to laminate

- **Graphics that will be repositioned often.** Lamination is a permanent finish; a graphic that needs to move or be re-applied should stay unlaminated or use a removable system.
- **Deeply textured or curved surfaces.** Standard film will not conform and will lift; confirm a conformable grade or choose another finish.
- **Floors, without a rated floor laminate.** Covered above — this one is about safety.
- **Prints whose ink has not fully cured.** Laminating over wet or gassing ink traps solvents under the film and causes bubbling. Let the print cure first — curing time depends on the ink system, so confirm it rather than guessing.
