One-way vision film (also called perforated film, or window perf) is the cleverest material in window advertising: from the street it reads as a complete full-colour image; from inside it is still a window. One piece of film does both jobs, and the trick is not magic — it is thousands of tiny holes. The image is printed on the solid part of the film; light and sight pass through the holes. Almost every decision about this film comes down to one number: **the perforation ratio**. This guide works through the four common ratios, when the one-way effect holds and when it fails, artwork, lamination and installation.

One premise first: "one-way" is not a property of the film itself — it is a product of **light conditions**. In daylight, when it is brighter outside than inside, passers-by see the printed image and people inside see out through the holes. At night, when the interior lights are brighter than the street, the effect reverses — people outside can glimpse inside, and the image goes dark. Accept that first, then choose a ratio.

## The short answer

For most retail storefronts, **60/40** is enough: it is the industry default, the image is solid enough and the view out is still usable. If the graphic matters more than the view — a high window, a display wall — choose **70/30**. If daylight and visibility matter most, or the film goes on a window in the driver's line of sight, choose **50/50**. Reserve 80/20 for display-only surfaces; it is not for vehicle windows. Each section below is that trade-off, expanded.

## 1. Perforation ratios: the four options side by side

The ratio is written "solid/open": 60/40 means 60% printable solid film and 40% open holes. Hole diameter is typically around 1.5 mm (slightly smaller, about 1.4 mm, on 70/30); confirm the actual pattern with your supplier.

| Ratio | Image from outside | View from inside | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50/50 | Lightest, colours look thin | Clearest, most open view | Vehicle windows (especially in the driver's sight lines), shops that need daylight |
| 60/40 | Solid, colours hold well | Usable, clear in daylight | **The default for retail storefronts** — most shops |
| 70/30 | More solid, close to solid vinyl | Darker, narrower view | High windows, display walls, promotion windows where the image comes first |
| 80/20 | Most solid, most detail | Noticeably darker | Display-only surfaces; **not for vehicle windows** |

The rule is simple: the more solid film, the better the image and the darker the view from inside. No ratio does everything; choosing one is deciding where to sit between "how good it looks from outside" and "how usable it is from inside".

One manufacturer-side note: high-ratio films (70/30, 80/20) are generally recommended for retail windows and point-of-purchase displays only, **not for vehicle windows**, because the reduced visibility is a safety issue. For vehicle windows, check your local road rules on window tinting and obstruction first — rules differ by jurisdiction, so confirm before ordering.

## 2. When the one-way effect works — and when it fails

The reverse side of perforated film is usually black. The black backing stops the unprinted side reflecting light inside, so the eye looks through the holes instead; it also makes the printed image read more solidly in daylight. That is the main structural difference from ordinary vinyl.

The effect needs three conditions:

- **Brighter outside than inside.** It works best in daylight; on overcast days and shaded elevations it weakens.
- **Not brighter inside than outside.** Once the shop lights come on at night, the effect reverses: outsiders can faintly see in, and the image darkens. If night-time privacy or a night-time image is a hard requirement, perforated film is not the answer.
- **The right viewing distance.** The image is made for people a few metres away. Up close, the holes interfere with the image — fine text and thin lines suffer most (see Section 4).

The film also cuts the light coming in: the higher the ratio, the darker the interior. 50/50 lets in the most light, 80/20 the least. If the shop relies on window daylight, or the window sits behind a seating or dining area, factor the light loss into the choice.

## 3. Lamination: should you add a clear protective film?

Perforated film can be finished with an optically clear protective laminate, which guards the print, resists scuffing and extends outdoor life. Whether to laminate depends on the job:

- **Long-term outdoor storefronts and vehicle windows: laminate.** The print never touches rain and washing directly, so it ages far more slowly.
- **Short promotions and interior glass partitions: skip it.** Lower cost, and easier to swap out.
- **Lamination slightly reduces the see-through.** One more layer means a slightly darker view from inside — if you are already at 70/30 or above, test a sample before committing.

Use a laminate made for perforated film; an ordinary laminate can block the holes, fog, or hurt the see-through. Your supplier should match the laminate to the film grade.

## 4. Artwork: design around the holes

The ratio dictates how the artwork must be made:

- **Fine text, thin lines and small logos "fall into" the holes.** With 30–50% of the surface open, thin strokes break up and small type goes missing. Make headlines and logos big and bold; move small print to solid vinyl or acrylic letters instead.
- **Design for distance.** A perforated image is read from 3–5 metres away by people walking past — lay it out for that distance, not for close-up poster reading.
- **Dark colours and high contrast work best.** The printable area is already discounted, so pale colours wash out in sunlight; a dark background with bright type is the safest bet on a storefront.
- **Leave a "sight zone".** The window in front of the till, or the one staff look through all day, can be left partly clear or done in a lower ratio — nobody wants to work all day behind a screen.

Before approving artwork, ask your supplier for a printed sample: the same design on 60/40 and on 70/30, stuck on the actual window. One look beats any specification sheet.

## 5. Installation and day-to-day care

- **Outside of the glass is the standard** (image facing out, black side in); film for inside application is a different product — state the application side when ordering.
- **Dry application is the norm.** Wet application is not recommended for perforated film with laminate: moisture gets trapped between the holes and the laminate and harms the see-through, and can leave marks after drying.
- **Clean the glass first.** Dust and grease make the edges around the holes lift; curved glass, rubber seals and non-glass surfaces are not suitable — adhesion fails there.
- **Clean with a soft cloth and a neutral cleaner.** No hard brushes or strong solvents on the print; the hole edges wear first.
- **Removal is normally one-way.** Once peeled off, the film is generally not reusable — price short seasonal campaigns as disposable; reserve the durable grades for long-term signage.

Outdoor service life varies widely with film grade, lamination, orientation and climate; your supplier should state the applicable life for your use — confirm it before ordering rather than borrowing another product's figure.

## Common mistakes

1. **Assuming the one-way effect works all day.** It reverses when the interior lights come on at night; plan night privacy separately.
2. **Designing only for the outside.** Staff look through it all day — covering a whole window in 80/20 is like hanging a screen across the shop.
3. **Setting small type too tight.** The holes eat the strokes; text breaks up close and blurs at distance.
4. **Copying a storefront ratio onto a vehicle.** High ratios do not belong on vehicle windows — check local rules first, then choose the ratio.
5. **Using ordinary laminate or a wet application.** Perforated film has its own laminate grades and dry-application method; getting either wrong hurts the see-through.

## When not to use perforated film

- **Where both sides need a clear view** — for example the lower half of a display window where customers must see the goods clearly. Use clear film there, or leave sections open.
- **When night privacy or a night image is essential.** Perforated film reverses at night; frosted film or blinds suit privacy better.
- **When the design is all fine text and small images.** The holes eat detail; solid vinyl or a lightbox is a better fit.
- **On curved surfaces, seals or non-glass materials.** It will not hold and the edges will lift.
- **For large wind-facing building hoarding.** Perforated film lets wind through, which is an advantage — but for full hoarding faces, mesh is usually the more economical choice; see the [PVC mesh fencing guide](/articles/pvc-mesh-construction-fencing-guide/) for the comparison.
